1. Overview: Acute Abdomen & Emergency GI Care

An "acute abdomen" refers to sudden, severe abdominal pain of recent onset that requires rapid clinical evaluation and urgent surgical intervention. Conditions such as acute appendicitis, perforated hollow viscus (stomach or intestinal perforation), and acute mechanical bowel obstruction can escalate rapidly into generalized peritonitis or sepsis if untreated.

Modern emergency surgical gastroenterology combines rapid hemodynamic resuscitation, precise emergency imaging, and round-the-clock minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic laparoscopy to resolve surgical emergencies safely with minimal patient trauma.

2. Symptoms & Warning Signs

Acute Appendicitis Signs

  • Pain beginning around the navel (periumbilical) that migrates to the right lower abdomen (McBurney point)
  • Sharp, worsening pain with coughing, walking, or sudden movements
  • Low-grade fever, loss of appetite (anorexia), and nausea
  • Localized abdominal tenderness and rebound tenderness in the right iliac fossa

Perforation & Obstruction Signs

  • Sudden, explosive "thunderclap" abdominal pain with widespread abdominal wall rigidity ("board-like" abdomen)
  • Inability to pass stools or flatus, accompanied by progressive abdominal distension
  • Repeated, bilious, or feculent vomiting
  • Rapid pulse, cold clammy extremities, and dizziness

3. When to Seek Urgent Clinical Care

Seek Immediate Evaluation If You Notice:

  • Sudden rigid, board-like abdomen with severe generalized pain: Signals perforated stomach or bowel ulcer with peritonitis requiring immediate emergency laparotomy or laparoscopy.
  • High fever, confusion, and falling blood pressure (Septic Shock): Life-threatening emergency requiring intensive care resuscitation and urgent surgical source control.
  • Complete cessation of flatus and stools with vomiting: Indicates acute intestinal obstruction or strangulation requiring immediate hospital admission.

4. Common Surgical Emergencies

Acute Appendicitis: Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen by a fecalith or lymphoid tissue leading to bacterial overgrowth, acute inflammation, ischemia, and potential perforation.

Gastrointestinal Perforation: A hole in the stomach, duodenum (peptic ulcer perforation), or intestine spilling digestive juices and bacteria into the peritoneal cavity, causing chemical and bacterial peritonitis.

Acute Mechanical Bowel Obstruction: Blockage of the small or large intestine caused by post-operative adhesions, incarcerated hernias, or volvulus.

Abdominal Trauma & Internal Hemorrhage: Blunt or penetrating injuries to solid organs (spleen, liver, mesentery) requiring emergency stabilization and surgical source control.

5. Potential Complications

  • Appendiceal Perforation & Abscess: Rupture of the inflamed appendix leading to localized phlegmon, pelvic abscess, or widespread peritonitis.
  • Bacterial Peritonitis: Severe infection of the peritoneal membrane with systemic endotoxemia.
  • Bowel Strangulation & Gangrene: Compromised mesenteric vascular inflow leading to irreversible bowel infarction.
  • Septic Shock & Multi-Organ Failure: Systemic inflammatory response to uncontrolled intra-abdominal infection.

6. Emergency Diagnostic Evaluation

  • High-Resolution Emergency Ultrasound: Frontline rapid scan identifying appendiceal diameter (>6mm), non-compressibility, appendicoliths, and free peritoneal fluid.
  • Non-Contrast / CECT Abdomen & Erect Chest X-ray: Essential to detect free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum in perforation) and multiple air-fluid levels in bowel obstruction.
  • Emergency Blood Panels: Leukocyte counts, arterial blood gases, serum lactate, and renal/electrolyte profiles to assess systemic infection severity.

7. Emergency Laparoscopic Surgery

Whenever clinically stable, emergency minimally invasive keyhole surgery provides rapid, safe resolution:

  • Laparoscopic Appendectomy: Removal of the inflamed appendix through 3 tiny keyhole incisions (5mm to 10mm). Allows thorough peritoneal lavage of the pelvis and right paracolic gutter, with patients discharged home within 24 to 48 hours.
  • Laparoscopic Perforation Closure (Graham Patch): Minimally invasive primary closure and omental patch repair for perforated duodenal or gastric ulcers, accompanied by extensive warm saline lavage.
  • Laparoscopic Adhesiolysis: Gentle release of obstructing bands and inspection of bowel viability under high-definition optical view.

8. Post-Operative Recovery

Following modern fast-track recovery protocols, intravenous antibiotics are de-escalated promptly, early oral sips are introduced as bowel sounds return, and mobilization begins on post-operative day one. Most patients recover uneventfully without long-term digestive sequelae.
